Alpha and Beta Decay Lab

AQA 7408 · 3.2.1.2 · stable and unstable nuclei
α decay: the nucleus emits 42α (2 protons + 2 neutrons). A falls by 4, Z falls by 2. Happens in large nuclei: the strong force cannot hold them together against the electrostatic repulsion of the protons.
β− decay: a neutron changes to a proton, n → p + e− + ν̄e. A stays the same, Z rises by 1. Happens in neutron-rich nuclei. The electron antineutrino carries away some of the energy.

If only two particles were produced, momentum and energy conservation would give the β− particle the same energy every time (one spike at 1.16 MeV). The measured energies are spread from 0 up to 1.16 MeV. In 1930 Pauli proposed a third, neutral, almost massless particle (the neutrino, here an antineutrino) that carries the rest of the energy. It was detected in 1956.

Learn the numbers: attractive from about 0.5 fm up to about 3 fm, repulsive below about 0.5 fm. The repulsion stops nucleons crushing together; the short range is why very large nuclei are unstable.
