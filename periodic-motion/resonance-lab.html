AQA 7408 · 3.6.1.4 · forced vibrations and resonance
The natural frequency here is f₀ = 1.00 Hz. The oscillator always vibrates at the driving frequency. Resonance: when f = f₀ the amplitude is a maximum, because energy is transferred most efficiently. More damping: lower peak, broader curve, and the peak shifts slightly below f₀.
All the light pendulums are forced at the driver's frequency. The one with the same length has the same natural frequency, so it resonates: biggest amplitude, and it lags the driver by π/2. Shorter pendulums (higher f₀) move nearly in phase with the driver; longer ones (lower f₀) move nearly in antiphase.

Useful resonance

Musical instruments: air columns and strings resonate at their harmonics (stationary waves).
Microwave ovens: water molecules absorb microwaves at a frequency that makes them oscillate strongly.
Radio tuning: a circuit is tuned so that it resonates at the station's frequency.
MRI scanners: nuclei absorb radio waves at their resonant frequency.

Resonance to avoid (add damping or change f₀)

Millennium Bridge, London (2000): walkers' sideways steps matched a natural frequency; fixed with dampers.
Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940): wind-driven oscillations grew until it collapsed (the mechanism was aerodynamic flutter, which is related to resonance).
Car parts and washing machines: panels or the drum rattle at certain engine or spin speeds; rubber mounts add damping.
Buildings in earthquakes: tuned mass dampers (for example in Taipei 101) absorb energy.
In exam answers: name the driver, the natural frequency it matches, and say how damping or changing the stiffness or mass reduces the amplitude.
